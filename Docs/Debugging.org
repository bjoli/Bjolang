#+TITLE: Debugging Bjolang programs
#+STARTUP: showall

What a .NET debugger can do with a Bjolang program today, how to set it up,
and what is known about what it cannot do yet.

* What the compiler gives a debugger

- The generated C# carries ~#line~ directives with full spans, line and
  column, back to the ~.bjo~ source. Code with no source of its own is
  ~#line hidden~.
- Every build writes a portable PDB beside its assembly, and the mapping is
  in it: the ~.bjo~ files are its documents, by full path, and its sequence
  points are their lines and columns. A stack trace's lines come from it.
- ~-d~ builds unoptimized (~DisableOptimizations~ in the assembly), which is
  what stepping wants. It writes nothing else; ~--emit-cs FILE~ writes the
  generated C#, ~--dump-ast FILE~ the typed AST, and ~--emit-cs-beside~ each
  input's C# beside it, which is what a batch takes.
- The locals in the PDB are renamed after the build (~PdbNames.fs~): a local
  the program wrote is shown under the name it wrote, ~is-big?~ rather than
  ~is_subbig_QMARK__4~, and the compiler's temporaries (~loopcur__75~,
  ~__init__65~, ~_dict_...~) are marked ~DebuggerHidden~. A local is the
  program's if its name, read back through the sanitizing and without its
  ~__N~ suffixes, was written in source the compilation read; an inlined
  library body's locals, ~elem~ from ~list-map~, count. The assembly's
  ~PdbChecksum~ is computed again over the patched PDB. Every build, not
  only ~-d~, since a debugged program steps into imports built without it.
  A patch that does not read back with the same tables and sequence points
  leaves Roslyn's files as they are; ~BJOLANG_RAW_PDB=1~ turns it off.
  ~TestFiles/pdb~ and the suite's debug-symbols phase check it.
- A local that is a field is renamed in the assembly by the same rule. A
  closure's captured locals are fields of a ~<>c__DisplayClass~, and an
  iterator's are fields of its state machine, ~<name>5__N~ — every local
  of a ~seql~, in a debug build, where Roslyn hoists them all. netcoredbg
  reads the name between ~<~ and ~>~, and leaves out a field whose name
  begins ~<>~; so the program's become ~is-big?~ and ~<next-pair>5__3~, and
  the compiler's ~<>v__N~ and ~<>h__N~. The assembly's string heap cannot
  grow, so a name is written over the old one, and only where it is no
  longer and shares its bytes with no other name. A captured parameter
  keeps its C# name: its field and the parameter are one string.

* Where fibers wait

A program that hangs usually has fibers that wait for each other. Two
things show where they wait. Neither needs a debugger.

** A deadline that stops the body says where the others were

When a ~with-deadline~ stops its body, the ~Cancelled~ that the body
raises says where every other fiber of the scope was waiting when the
deadline passed. Fibers in nested scopes are counted too:

#+BEGIN_SRC
cancelled: (Deadline)
When the deadline passed, 4 other fibers were waiting:
  3 fibers  at app.bjo:14  (ignore (sync (chan-recv jobs)))
  1 fiber   at app.bjo:21  (sync (timeout (minutes 1)))
#+END_SRC

This is always on. It costs nothing until a deadline stops a fiber,
and the text is made from the stacks of the cancellations only when the
message is read. A deadline that stops only children, after the body has
returned, is not a failure, so it raises nothing and there is no report.

** The fiber trace: ~BJO_FIBER_TRACE=1~

Set ~BJO_FIBER_TRACE~ in the environment when the program starts:

#+BEGIN_SRC sh
BJO_FIBER_TRACE=1 dotnet app.exe
#+END_SRC

The program then keeps a record of each fiber that waits. You get the
record as a report in three ways:

- ~(fiber-trace)~ answers it as a string.
- SIGQUIT (Ctrl-\ in a terminal, or ~kill -QUIT pid~) writes it to
  standard error. The program does not stop.
- When the program looks deadlocked, it writes the report once by itself.
  That is when nothing has run for two seconds and every fiber waits on a
  channel, on another fiber or on the close of a scope.

#+BEGIN_SRC
Bjolang fiber trace: nothing has run for 2 s, and every fiber waits on a channel or on another fiber. This looks like a deadlock.
Bjolang fiber trace: 3 fibers waiting.

main

  scope, opened at app.bjo:25  (with-scope

    scope, opened at app.bjo:18  (with-scope
      1 fiber closing this scope, waiting for its children, for 2.5 s
          at   app.bjo:18  (with-scope
          from app.bjo:28  (table f1 f2))
      1 fiber waiting in chan-recv on channel #1, for 2.4 s
          at   app.bjo:9  (def r (sync (chan-recv right)))
      1 fiber waiting in chan-recv on channel #2, for 2.4 s
          at   app.bjo:9  (def r (sync (chan-recv right)))
#+END_SRC

Each fiber is under the scope it waits in, and the scopes are in a tree.
The ~at~ line is the ~sync~. The ~from~ lines are the bjoroutines that
called it, innermost first. Fibers that wait at the same place, on the
same thing, are counted together. A channel has a number, so two fibers
that wait on one channel can be seen to do so. A thread that waits in
~sync/blocking~ or at the close of a scope is in the report too.

The deadlock check is careful not to report a program that can still go
on. A wait on a timer, a ~choose~, an inbox or a .NET task can end without
help from a fiber, so a program with one of those waits is not reported.
Nor is a program whose ~defun~ ~main~ still runs, or one whose scheduler
still has work. A program that is stuck in such a wait is shown by
SIGQUIT.

*** What it costs

With the trace off, nothing. Every hook tests ~FiberTrace.Enabled~, a
~static readonly~ field, and the JIT removes the test and the code after
it when it recompiles a hot method. On ~bench/bjolang/cmlbench.bjo~ the
rings, the spawn burst and the chooses run at the same speed and allocate
the same, with the hooks and without them.

With the trace on, each suspension records the frame in a table, and the
first suspension at each ~sync~ reads the stack once to find the line.
The ring of ~cmlbench~ goes from about 70 to about 260 ns a message. Use
it to find where a program is stuck, not in production.

*** What it does not show

- A fiber that runs, or is queued to run, has no place: it shows as
  "running, or ready to run".
- A fiber in a pure loop never waits, so the deadlock check sees a
  scheduler with work and says nothing.
- Without a pdb there are no lines, only method names.

* Setting it up

- [[https://github.com/Samsung/netcoredbg/releases][netcoredbg]], unpacked to ~~/.local/share/netcoredbg~. It speaks the Debug
  Adapter Protocol.
- In Emacs, ~dape~ with ~emacs/bjolang-dape.el~ from BjolangLsp. ~M-x dape
  RET bjolang RET~ in a file with a ~main~ builds it with ~-d~ and runs it
  under netcoredbg.
- A Bjolang program's ~app.exe~ is a managed assembly, not a native host,
  so netcoredbg is told to launch ~dotnet~ with the program as its argument.
  Launched as the program itself it fails with ~0x80070002~, file not found.
- netcoredbg's ~--interpreter=cli~ cannot be driven by piping commands into
  it: they do not wait for the program to stop. Its DAP mode is what works.

* What works

Checked with netcoredbg 3.2.0, through a DAP client and through ~dape~ in a
batch Emacs, on a small program:

- A breakpoint set in the ~.bjo~ file is pending, and binds when the module
  loads.
- A stop is at the right ~.bjo~ line and column, and the call stack names
  them: ~totalsubof() app.bjo:7~ above ~main() app.bjo:11~.
- Stepping goes by ~.bjo~ lines.
- The locals are the program's, under its names: ~xs~, ~doubled~, and in an
  inlined loop ~acc~, ~elem~, ~x~. A value expands into its fields, so a list
  can be walked one cell at a time.
- A string's text is its ~DebugText~, second when it is expanded:
  ~greeting = {BjoString.Utf8String}~, and under it ~DebugText = "hej på
  dig"~, for a slice too. netcoredbg runs a value's property getters when it
  is expanded, and lists them in the order they are declared.

* What does not, yet

- *Stepping enters the runtime.* Under Just My Code netcoredbg counts every
  module whose PDB is loaded as user code, optimized or not; Visual Studio
  would leave the optimized runtime out, netcoredbg does not. The runtime's
  PDBs are beside its assemblies, so a step from the end of a function, which
  lands in hidden code of its caller and keeps stepping for a source line,
  stops in ~BjoNum.cs~. With the runtime's PDBs moved aside the same steps
  stay in the ~.bjo~ file, and netcoredbg does honour
  ~[DebuggerNonUserCode]~: a test library's marked class is stepped over
  and its unmarked one entered. The attribute cannot be put on an assembly,
  and the runtime has some 800 types. Ways out:
  1. stamp the attribute on every type of each runtime assembly after its
     build, with Mono.Cecil, keeping the PDBs and their line numbers;
  2. ship the release runtime without PDBs, which loses the line numbers of
     runtime frames in a stack trace;
  3. write the attribute in the source, on every type, in two repositories.
  The standard library has the same question: its modules are optimized
  and have PDBs, so step-into enters ~prelude.bjo~. The compiler could emit
  the attribute on a module's class when the module is the standard
  library's; whether netcoredbg applies it to closures, which C# puts in
  nested compiler-generated classes, is not known.
- *Values are their C# structure.* A list is ~{SchemeList.Cons<int>}~ and
  expands to ~_car~, ~_cdr~, ~Head~, ~Tail~, each value twice. netcoredbg
  reads neither ~DebuggerDisplay~ nor ~DebuggerTypeProxy~, nor
  ~DebuggerBrowsable~ (checked: a field marked ~Never~ is still listed), so
  a string's line is ~{BjoString.Utf8String}~ and its text is a property
  under it (see above); SharpDbg, a DAP
  debugger written in C#, reads both, and so does Rider. With one of those,
  the generated union and record classes could carry a ~DebuggerDisplay~
  in their Bjolang form, and the runtime collections a ~DebuggerTypeProxy~.
- *Function and parameter names are C#'s:* ~totalsubof~, ~intsubgtstring~.
  A parameter is named in the assembly's metadata, not in the PDB, so the
  rename does not reach it.
- *Some sequence points have impossible columns,* such as column 61 on a
  line of 20 characters, probably from inlined or monomorphised code
  carrying another range's columns. None was seen to cause a wrong stop.
- *Frames of the prelude show no source* (~?:0~), possibly because the code
  at that point is optimized or hidden.
- *The watch and the REPL evaluate C#,* not Bjolang, so they take C#
  spellings of names.
- *A suspended bjoroutine's stack* is in its continuations, so the call stack
  ends at the scheduler. The fiber trace (see "Where fibers wait") puts
  the chain of a waiting fiber back together, with its lines.
- *A breakpoint on a seql's last clause* can bind in the loop's exit
  function, ~<main>g__loopexit__N~, rather than in the iterator, and there
  netcoredbg fails to list the locals (~variables~ answers ~0x80004005~). A
  breakpoint on an earlier clause stops in the iterator. Seen too: a
  breakpoint on a line below a lambda stopping in the lambda, as if its
  sequence points ran a line long.
